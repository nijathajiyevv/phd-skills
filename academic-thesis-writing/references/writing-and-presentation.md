# Writing, register and presentation balance

Readability of scientific prose has declined steadily, driven by general scientific jargon rather than necessary technical terms (Plavén-Sigray et al. 2017). A thesis is read selectively by tired experts; clarity is a scoring advantage.

## Register

- Keep technical terms, define them at first use, one term per concept for the whole thesis.
- Cut tone jargon ("utilise", "leverage", "robust" without evidence) and throat-clearing.
- Calibrate hedges and boosters to evidence; interpretation in the discussion carries most hedging.
- First person where the discipline and supervisor accept it, especially in qualitative and reflexive work.
- Signpost more than in an article: chapter openings and closings, section previews, cross-references to earlier chapters.
- Language background: examiners tolerate idiosyncratic non-native grammar more than sloppiness. Prioritise correctness and consistency over native-sounding prose, and preserve the candidate's voice.

## Craft

- Paragraphs: topic sentence, development, takeaway.
- Old information before new within sentences; subject near verb.
- Parallel points in parallel form; related ideas adjacent.
- Rewrite stuck paragraphs instead of patching.

## Visual balance

- Each finding has one home: text, table, figure or appendix.
- Long results chapters need displays to carry patterns; walls of numbers in prose are flagged.
- Displays stand alone: number, title, caption, units, abbreviations, source.
- Consistent colours, group order and notation across all chapters.

## Logical balance

Each analytical paragraph carries claim, evidence and reasoning. Each chapter states its job at the start and its result at the end, and that result feeds the next chapter.

## Procedure

Two separate passes: clarity (reverse outline, definitions, sentence fixes, jargon list) then register (hedging, formality, voice). Run a terminology-consistency and jargon report across the whole thesis, not chapter by chapter.
