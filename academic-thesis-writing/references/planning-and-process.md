# Planning and writing process

Completion is not the default outcome. In the US PhD Completion Project, history reached only about 48% completion by year ten and several social sciences approached 45%, while most science fields exceeded 60%. Planning and writing-process failures, not intellect, drive most late or abandoned theses.

## Planning

1. Reduce the project to a researchable question, sub-questions, and the claimed contribution type. Revisit every three months.
2. Write from month one: methods notes, reading notes in the candidate's own words, a running argument document. Writing is how thinking becomes examinable.
3. Backward plan from the submission date. Typical buffers (adjust to regulations and supervisor turnaround): final proofreading about 2 weeks; supervisor full-draft read 4 to 6 weeks; integration and introduction/conclusion rewrite 6 to 8 weeks; chapter drafts before that.
4. Agree with the supervisor in writing: meeting frequency, draft turnaround, what "draft" means, authorship on papers.
5. Keep a decision log (choice, date, reason, alternatives rejected). It writes the methodology chapter and answers viva questions.
6. Use a reference manager from the first paper read.
7. Read regulations early: format, word limit, by-publication eligibility, AI policy, embargo and data deposit rules.

Planner output:

```
| Chapter (candidate's numbering) | Status | Draft due | Supervisor read | Revision due | Risk |
```

Flag any chapter without a draft date.

## Writing process

1. Maintain the one-page argument (question, claim, one sentence per chapter). Update it when a chapter changes.
2. Draft fast, revise slowly; separate generating from editing.
3. Give each chapter a short opening (its job for the question) and closing (what it established, what the next chapter needs).
4. Reverse-outline each finished chapter: one line per paragraph; move or cut paragraphs that do not serve the chapter's job.
5. Ask supervisors for one feedback level per draft: argument, structure, or line edits.
6. Track feedback in a table (comment, response, change) so recurring issues show.
7. Before the final supervisor read, read the assembled thesis for coherence, repetition and terminology drift only.
8. Use copy-editing only within university rules; editors and AI tools must not change substance, argument or interpretation.

## Anti-patterns to name directly

- Delaying writing until "the results are in"
- Perfecting chapter 1 before drafting later chapters
- Writing the introduction first and never rewriting it
- Expanding scope after the mid-candidature review
- Sending 80-page drafts without saying what feedback is wanted
- Treating supervisor silence as approval; hiding problems
- "I need to read more before I can write" (usually avoidance)
- "It just needs polishing" (usually means the argument is unsettled)
