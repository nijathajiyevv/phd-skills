# Examiners and the defence

## How examiners read (Mullins & Kiley 2002, 30 experienced examiners)

- They tend to start expecting the thesis to pass; an inadequate literature review triggers closer scrutiny of methods and analysis.
- Reading is non-linear: abstract, introduction, conclusion and references first. These bookends set the verdict frame.
- Examiner experience mattered even more than methodological orientation (Mewburn reads this as experienced examiners being more forgiving); examiner selection matters.
- Sloppiness (typos, missing references, badly formatted bibliographies) irritates examiners in every discipline; non-native grammar is tolerated more than mess.
- The most common complaint in examiner reports (per Mewburn's experience of reports) is that the examiner did not understand what the candidate was trying to do, usually about method.

Implications for the skill: polish the bookends and the reference list hardest; make the method rationale impossible to misread; discuss examiner nominations and exclusions with the supervisor where allowed.

## Viva or defence preparation

Examiners want to be convinced the work is original, the candidate's own, and a contribution worthy of the degree. Expect questions on originality, significance and rigour.

1. Re-read the thesis 1 to 2 weeks before; prepare a corrections list.
2. Prepare 2-minute and 10-minute summaries: question, contribution, method rationale, main findings, limitations.
3. Prepare answers to the standard set: why this question; why this method over alternatives; what is original; what would you do differently; limitations; relation to the examiners' own work; what next.
4. Read the examiners' recent publications and new work in the field since submission.
5. Hold a mock viva with someone willing to be hostile.
6. In the room: answer the question asked, briefly; ask to clarify compound questions; concede genuine weaknesses and explain their bounds.

Don't defend every point; don't memorise scripts; don't treat corrections as failure (most outcomes include corrections).

## Viva-trainer procedure

1. Extract from the thesis: contribution claims, major method choices, limitations, unexpected findings, any "first" claims.
2. Generate questions from each, plus the standard set.
3. Ask one at a time; after each answer, ask a follow-up probing the weakest part.
4. Feedback per answer: directness, evidence used, quality of concession, length.
5. End with the three weakest areas to prepare further.

Questions come from the candidate's own thesis, never generic lists alone.
